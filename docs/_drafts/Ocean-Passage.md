I sailed out of the Whale Cut to leave the sea of Abaco, pass the
Bahamas barrier reef and find the deep, blue waters of the Atlantic Ocean
northeast of the Bahamas shelf.

The blue of the Atlantic is nothing like that of the Pacific. The Pacific
blue is a saphire blue. The Atlantic blue is a deep indigo. It is what we
call "navy blue". Looking into the water 340 fathoms deep
there is no sense of anything but a surface. The water is liquid blue as
opaque as ink. In your mind you know it is crystal clear water. Your eyes
tell you that it is not. It is ink.

The next morning I'm sixty miles north of the Bahamas bank. I encounter
some ships coming out of the Florida straights and heading out to the east,
or the other direction, from the east for the Florida straights. My early
in the morning arrival to this shipping lane deprives me of some sleep.

I did a little motoring because the wind on the quarter wasn't strong
enough to keep the sails full when the waves rolled the boat. Sometimes
the wind matches the waves. The staysail with the right amount of main sail
will keep the boat heeled over the waves, so that the mast moving back
and forth does not flog them. Other times, not. Then I'll drop the staysail.

If the wind dies enough that the main starts flogging I can try taking-out
a reef or I can give-up and motor. The boat will not sail with the wind closer
than forty-five degrees to the stern. The sails won't hold full.
With only the main held-out by a preventer, bringing the wind closer to
the stern creates too much weather helm. I've tried everything to sail
more closely downwind. Acceptance is the solution.

It's hard to describe how beautiful it is. So blue. Like that big blue marble,
that little blue marble in space that moon passage astronauts talk about. It's
difficult to communicate the beauty of it. It's almost a shame to see it alone,
by myself, not to share it. Yet, I think being alone just makes it a little bit
more poignant, as you are, here you are, alone by yourself, out here, in the
middle of the ocean, on a little boat, sailing along.

You'd have to be somebody that wasn't afraid. I mean, not any more than the
normal afraid.  I was thinking about that.  I have this feeling in my stomach,
in my chest, when I go on a passage. I was trying to identify what it is. It's
not exactly fear, because there's wonder in it. It's more, it's more like,
and...  I don't know. I feel like I should reject this idea, yet at the same
time, it might be right. It's similar to the feeling you have when you fall in
love with somebody.  It is like, "oh, this is really great." And, "oh, this is
really scary." And, "oh, this is really happening." Very similar to that. So,
there you go. It's like falling in love.

The inner stay parted from the bowsprit because the nut underneath had worked
its way off of the bolt. Thank goodness this happened when the wind was light.
The sail was not heavily loaded. In fact, it was me that pulled it finally
loose when I grabbed it for support while coming off of the bowsprit, where
I was changing head sails.

This will have to be an inspection item. Fortunately, I had more of the correct
size bolts.  This repair at sea got not only a lock washer but a second, self
locking nut to back the first nut. Both of those nuts got a dose of permanent
thread lock.  Hopefully I have done a better job. In any case, I know what to
look for now when the inner stay starts to slack.

The third day I motored all day in the gulf stream almost a hundred miles
off of the coast of Florida. The track from PredictWind keeps me in the
strongest current. I'm seeing almost nine knots at times. It would be better
to tarry here, enjoying three free nautical miles every hour. I'm in a hurry.
The forecast has rough conditions at my destination, Beaufort, NC, only
hours after my predicted arrival. I feel like I'm cutting it a little finely.

When the ocean is settled it looks like a vast prarie of rolling hills.
Instead of going over the hills and through the valleys, the hills and
valleys come to you. Everywhere in every direction there is blue water all of
the way to the horizon. It feels like an impossible amount of water. It is
unbelievably flat yet subtly undulating and textured, liquid. Every part is
calm. Every part is in motion.

There are flying fish from time to time. They skip along the tops of the
waves, moving faster than even the strongest fish could swim, glinting silver.
In the morning I had three that had flown blindly onto the deck. Poor things.
If I could stomach eating fish I would fry them up for a mid-morning snack.
The life of a fish: you need to eat and there's always something trying to
eat you.

I've been watching a catamaran all day motoring along with me. They have
their main sail flying. That makes them easy to spot. At last I hail them with
the VHF radio.  They're going where I'm going. At the end of the day I decide
not to sweat the arrival at Beaufort. I decide to divert to Charleston, SC. It
is a day closer.  My companion boat will continue. They'll speed up and make it
without sweating the weather.

Before sunset, with the sea still calm, I stop the boat, tie the tiller over,
and take a bath in the gulf stream waters. It's easy to know that you are
in the current. Other than the nearly three knot ground vector, the water
is noticably warmer.

Late in the evening the wind picks up again. I'm able to sail overnight and all
of the next day to Charleston. A small, solitary dolphin joins me, taking a
boost from my bow wake. I see it for the last time as I'm taking down sail
outside of the harbor.  The sun sets on the fourth day as I motor into the
anchorage. I've sailed four hundred and fifty-two nautical miles.
